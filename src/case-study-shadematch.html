ShadeMatch · Product Design Case Study · 2026

The best decision I made was a feature I didn't ship.

I built ShadeMatch because I kept running late for my bus, unable to pick an outfit. It looks at one piece of clothing you own and tells you what goes with it. This is how I decided what it should do, and what it shouldn't.

01 / The Problem

A full wardrobe, nothing to wear.

I lived this. It's a design problem.

Most of us own far more than we wear. It's not bad shopping. We just can't see what goes with what. A shirt stays in the cupboard because nobody ever showed us what to pair it with.

So we repeat the same five outfits, and buy a new top because the old one "goes with nothing".

Nobody thinks "I have an information problem." They think "I'm just bad at clothes."

So nobody is searching for this app. There is no query for “help me see what goes with my green shirt.” It has to prove itself in the first thirty seconds, before anyone agrees they have a problem.

It works, and it's live. But building something and understanding it are two different things, and I had five questions I couldn't answer.

  • ?Who is this for, specifically?
  • ?What is the moment they'd open it?
  • ?Why would they open it a second time?
  • ?Which of these five features is the product, and which are decoration?
  • ?What would I cut?

The brief, written after the fact

Help people wear more of what they already own by answering one question, what goes with this? In under thirty seconds, with no setup.

Success isn't people using the app more. It's a shirt finally leaving the shelf.

02 / The Solution

One item in. An answer out.

Everything else either helped with that one job or got in its way. Working out which was which was the real design work.

01

Single item capture

Photograph one garment. That's the whole input. No uploading your wardrobe, no tagging.

02

Three colours per piece

Photograph a shirt and you get three colours for trousers and three for shoes. The men or women setting shifts the palette.

Trousers
Shoes
03

Visualise before committing

The Pinterest link answers the question a colour swatch can't: what does this look like on a person? It's a quick way from a colour to a real outfit.

04

Save for later

An optional account lets you save a combination, so next time you already know what goes with that top. Nothing is required to use the app without it.

Next on my list: the marketplace links

Amazon, Myntra and Flipkart links still sit beside every result. My research says they make good advice feel like an ad, so taking them out is the first thing I'd change. The reasoning is in Ideation.

03 / Research / Competitive Analysis

Setup first, or not your clothes.

Four kinds of product try to help. The fifth option is no product at all. What's missing is one that answers the question at the moment you ask it.

A

Wardrobe apps

Looked at: Whering, Acloset, Indyx, Stylebook

The most complete answer. Add your whole wardrobe and the app builds outfits from what you own.

The gap

The setup comes first. Photographing and tagging a whole wardrobe takes hours, before you get a single answer.

B

Pinterest

Looked at: Pinterest search and boards

Unmatched for inspiration, and the only place that shows what a colour combination looks like on a real person.

The gap

It's all clothes you don't own. It shows what looks good, never what you can wear tonight. That gap is what ShadeMatch is for.

C

Marketplace styling

Looked at: Myntra “Complete the Look”, Amazon “Style with”

Good recommendations, shown right when you're ready to buy.

The gap

Every suggestion is something for sale. The app isn't asking "what goes with this?", it's asking "what else can we sell this person?" Shoppers know it, and a suggestion sitting next to a buy button gets trusted less, even when it's right.

D

General AI chat

Looked at: ChatGPT and Gemini with a photo

Free, immediate, capable. Upload a photo of a shirt, ask what goes with it, get a reasonable answer. The strongest competitor in the category and the least discussed.

The gap

Nothing persists. No memory of your wardrobe, no record of yesterday's question, no visual output, just text. It also needs the user to know they can ask, and to phrase it well. A capable tool with no product around it.

Same underlying intelligence I use myself. The difference isn't the answer, it's that you never have to ask the question well, you just point the camera.

04 / Research / Voices

I read what people said about the wardrobe apps they quit.

Method

No interviews for this one. I used two sources instead.

Source 1

Community threads

AI pulled threads from fashion advice communities. I looked for how people describe the moment of not knowing what goes with something.

Source 2

App reviews

AI pulled the 2 and 3 star reviews of the wardrobe apps I looked at, from the App Store and Play Store. I read through them. This is where people explain why they quit.

2 and 3 starThe real reasons
1 and 5 starNoise
What I got

Honest opinions at volume, and a clear view of why competitors lose people.

What I missed

No follow-up questions. Only people who bothered to post. The quiet majority who used an app happily never show up.

Three clusters

Cluster 01

“I don't trust my own eye.”

Illustrative, based on patterns across the reviews and threads I read
What I saw

The main feeling is self doubt, not confusion. People feel they are missing a skill everyone else has. They own clothes they like and have never worn, and can't say why.

What they need

→A second opinion with a reason. Not a verdict, an explanation they can carry forward.

Cluster 02  ★ Key insight

“I scanned in over 60 pairs of shoes and 125 dresses alone! Not to mention about 20 tops when I stopped scanning because the try this outfit feature stopped working!”

What I saw

Mostly from review mining. People start excited, upload fifteen to thirty items, then quit before the app has done anything useful. The work comes before the value, every time.

Excited  →  15 to 30 items uploaded  →  Quits
What they need

→Value at item one, not item one hundred.

Cluster 03  ★ Key insight

“Everything is trying to sell me something.”

Illustrative, based on patterns across the reviews and threads I read
What I saw

A weary suspicion of styling advice attached to commerce. People discount recommendations automatically when a buy button is present, not because the advice is wrong but because they can't tell whether it's for them or for the retailer.

What they need

→Advice with no motive. The recommendation has to be visibly disinterested to be usable at all. This cluster directly contradicts a decision in the original build.

Redefined challenge

My first version treated this as a colour problem and solved it with a colour formula. It isn't one. It's a confidence problem. People don't just need an answer, they need an answer they understand.

How might we help people wear more of what they own, by giving them an instant second opinion on a single garment, one that explains itself, needs no setup, and has nothing to sell them?
Built for: the moment of getting dressed, holding one item Not for: wardrobe management, shopping, or building a styling habit
06 / Design / Ideation

The hardest part was subtraction.

Most design projects start with nothing and add. This one started full. ShadeMatch already did five things, and my job was to decide which were the product and which were just clutter.

The biggest cut was the wardrobe upload: it would have made ShadeMatch the same as the wardrobe apps.

The three questions everything had to pass

5features as built
4pass, 1 fails
01

Does this serve the moment of getting dressed? Not shopping, not browsing. If a feature's best use case was “while browsing Myntra,” it was out of the core however well it worked.

02

Does it cost the user anything before it gives them something? Setup cost is what kills this category. Accounts, uploads, quizzes and onboarding were out unless they earned their cost inside the same session.

03

Does it survive the trust test? Would Aditi believe this recommendation if she knew exactly how the product made money? If not, it was damaging the product even while working correctly.

The audit below is these three gates applied to what shipped. One feature failed outright and is next to change.

Why the marketplace links are the next thing I would change

The one decision I haven't acted on yet, so here's the reasoning.

The case for keeping them

They make it easy to act: tap a colour and go straight to buying it. They're useful too, because only you know what is in your wardrobe. If you don't own the colour it suggests, the links are how you find it.

The case for removing them

Cluster 3 is unambiguous. Trust here isn't a nice to have, it is the product.

The entire reason Aditi would choose this over Myntra's “Complete the Look” is the absence of a motive.

It would snowball if I ever added affiliate earnings. Once money depends on clicks, every later decision starts leaning toward the click. Removing the links first takes that pressure away before it starts.

What I would do: move them behind a deliberate tap, so you only see them when you ask. Then seeing what exists commercially is a service rather than an ad, because the user asked. I would test it with the commerce A/B in Measuring success before committing.

07 / Design / Evaluation

Checking my app against research.

ShadeMatch had no recruited testers behind this section either. What I ran was a structured self evaluation, walking through the live app screen by screen and checking it against the research findings from the competitive analysis, the voices, and the personas.

Method

Three questions per screen:

01What would a first time user expect before opening this?
02Could they say what the app just told them, in their own words?
03Would they actually wear it? Not “is this correct,” but “did it change what someone would do.”
To be honest about it: this was me checking my own app against my research, not a test with real users. It's good at catching confusing screens. It can't tell me whether anyone's habits changed. That needs the diary study below.

What it caught

The evaluation didn't change what the product is. It changed three things about how it has to behave.

The output gives an answer with no reason attached.

A combination arrives with no explanation, which doesn't help a user whose real problem is confidence.

Suggest colours that match and say why in one line. Every output must teach, not just answer

The shopping links change how the whole app reads.

Once you see them, every suggestion looks like a sales pitch. This is exactly what Cluster 3 warned me about, and I only saw it by looking at my own app like a stranger.

Help users match colours to clothes they already own. Commerce only on request, never volunteered

A swatch doesn't answer “what does that look like on me.”

The Pinterest link, treated as a minor extra, is the only thing that actually answers this.

Let them see it on a person before committing. Abstract palettes don't convert to worn outfits
08 / Design / The Flow

One photo to an answer.

The whole product is one short loop. Aditi is at her cupboard with two minutes, and every extra screen is a chance to lose her.

01 Capture
02 Detect
03 Recommend
04 Visualise
The whole product in one row. Four screens, from photo to answer.
Step 01

Capture

What happens

You land on one screen. Take a photo with your camera, or drag and drop an image. No home screen, no login, no setup.

The design decision

No account until someone has a reason to want one. Every extra screen before the photo loses people, especially the ones who don't yet think they have a problem. Landing straight on the photo screen says: this costs nothing to try.

What it protects

The only structural advantage over wardrobe apps, and worth more than everything an account would enable.

Step 02

Detection

What happens

The garment's dominant colour is identified and shown back, named rather than just swatched. “Rust orange.” “Sage.”

The design decision

Show what the app saw before it recommends anything. If it got the garment wrong, everything after is wrong, and the user should catch that early. Naming the colour helps too. A user who learns her shirt is “rust”, not “orange-ish”, keeps that.

What it protects

Trust. When an AI app shows its work where that's easy, people believe it more when it can't.

Step 03

Recommendation

What happens

Three colours for each piece that goes with the one you photographed.

The decision: three colours

Three colours is a recommendation. A long list of schemes is a colour picker.

Step 04

Visualise

What happens

One tap opens Pinterest, already searched for the specific combination, showing real people wearing it.

What I planned, and what I shipped

The original plan was to show both garments together in one image, so you could actually see the combination instead of imagining it.

I hit technical issues getting that to work reliably.

So I fell back to something simpler: a direct link to Pinterest, showing real people wearing that exact colour combination. Not as tailored as a custom generated image. But faster to ship, and it gets the person to a real picture today.

The planNever built
One image showing both garments together.
technical issues
What shipped
A link that opens Pinterest, already searched for the combination.
The risk, acknowledged

Sending people to another app means some won't come back. That's the price of the fallback. Getting the image inside ShadeMatch is the next thing I'd fix.

09 / Evaluation / Measuring Success

The easy number was the wrong one.

The easiest thing to count is clicks to Amazon, Myntra and Flipkart. It's exact and it ties straight to money. And chasing it would undo everything that makes the app work.

North star metric

The one number that matters most
Out of people who come back, how many end with someone wearing something they would not otherwise have worn?
I don't countVisits · Time in the app · Number of suggestions

Those all go up when an app is confusing. The goal is a shirt leaving the cupboard.

The honest catch

I can't see what someone actually wears. So the best stand-in is one optional tap when they come back: “Did you wear it?” Asking costs a little effort, and this app promises no effort. So I would only ask on a return visit, and never block anything. I'd accept some missing answers to keep it effortless. It's a trade-off I'd want to test.

How I'd validate

Qual
Watch people use it

5 to 8 people who match Aditi, testing on their own wardrobe. Does the reason change whether they'd wear it? Do they even notice the shopping links are gone?

Longitudinal
Track it for two weeks

6 people keep a diary of what they actually wore. It's the only way to know if clothes people owned but never wore actually got worn.

Quant
Test the shopping links

Links shown automatically against links shown only when asked for, measured by whether people trust the suggestion, not by clicks. The decision I'd most want proven right or wrong.

11 / Reflection

What the audit taught me.

  1. 01
    I had an edge and didn't know it.
    Why

    ShadeMatch's strongest point is that it only asks for one photo, which is exactly what its competitors get wrong. But I never chose that. I stumbled into it. You can't tell getting it right by luck from getting it right on purpose until someone asks you why. The research turned a lucky accident into a choice I can defend, and I know not to give it up.

  2. 02
    The same feature is helpful or manipulative depending on who starts the conversation.
    Why

    Shopping links beside a recommendation are an ad. The identical links, shown only when the user asks, are a service. Only the initiator changed. I used to evaluate features on what they do. Now the first question is who asked for it, because that sets the meaning more than the function does.

  3. 03
    Success here means the user needs me less.
    Why

    I never set out to teach Aditi colour theory, I set out to help her wear what she owns. The two pull in different directions: the more she learns, the less she needs me. The honest answer isn't to make her dependent, it's to widen what the app helps with as her wardrobe, seasons and occasions change, so she keeps coming back for new problems, not the one she's already solved.

I built the colour matching twice, hand-coded first, then with the Gemini API to compare. The AI version wasn't more accurate, just less reliable over time, so the simpler version stayed as the real engine.

What I'd do next

The gap between this case study and a real product is one study: two weeks, six people, a diary of what they actually wore. Everything here is reasoning, and reasoning is cheap.

The second thing I'd do is audit the colour engine itself. Writing this, I noticed I'd spent weeks interrogating every decision around the recommendation and none on whether the recommendation is any good.
