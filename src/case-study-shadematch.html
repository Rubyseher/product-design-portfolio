ShadeMatch · Product Design Case Study · 2026

The best decision I made was a feature I didn't build.

I built ShadeMatch because I kept running late for my bus, unable to pick an outfit. It looks at one piece of clothing you own and tells you what goes with it. This is how I decided what it should do, and what it shouldn't.

What I learned
01 / The Problem

A full wardrobe and nothing to wear. I lived that every morning, and it's a design problem, not a joke.

Most of us own far more than we wear. It's not bad shopping. We just can't see what goes with what. A shirt stays in the cupboard because nobody ever showed us what to pair it with.

So we repeat the same five outfits, and buy a new top because the old one "goes with nothing".

Nobody thinks "I have an information problem." They think "I'm just bad at clothes."

So nobody is searching for this app. There is no query for “help me see what goes with my green shirt.” It has to prove itself in the first thirty seconds, before anyone agrees they have a problem.

What ShadeMatch was, before any of this

ShadeMatch does five things. Here they are, in the order you meet them.

  1. 1

    Photograph a garment

    Take a photo or drop one in. The app finds the garment and its colour.

  2. 2

    Colour combinations

    Three colours for each piece that goes with it. A shirt gets three for trousers and three for shoes.

  3. 3

    Men or women

    Men's colours run darker. Women's run lighter and more playful.

  4. 4

    Shopping links

    Amazon, Myntra and Flipkart links appear with every result.

  5. 5

    Pinterest button

    Opens a search for the combination, so you can see it on real people.

It works, and it's live. But building something and understanding it are two different things, and I had five questions I couldn't answer.

  • ?Who is this for, specifically?
  • ?What is the moment they'd open it?
  • ?Why would they open it a second time?
  • ?Which of these five features is the product, and which are decoration?
  • ?What would I cut?

The brief, written after the fact

Help people wear more of what they already own by answering one question, what goes with this? In under thirty seconds, with no setup.

Success isn't people using the app more. It's a shirt finally leaving the shelf.

02 / The Solution

One item in. A wearable answer out. No setup.

Everything else either helped with that one job or got in its way. Working out which was which was the real design work.

01

Single item capture

Photograph one garment. That's the whole input. No uploading your wardrobe, no tagging.

02

Three colours per piece

Photograph a shirt and you get three colours for trousers and three for shoes. The men or women setting shifts the palette.

Trousers
Shoes
03

Visualise before committing

The Pinterest link answers the question a colour swatch can't: what does this look like on a person? It's a quick way from a colour to a real outfit.

04

Save for later

An optional account lets you save a combination, so next time you already know what goes with that top. Nothing is required to use the app without it.

Next on my list: the marketplace links

Amazon, Myntra and Flipkart links still sit beside every result. My research says they make good advice feel like an ad, so taking them out is the first thing I'd change. The reasoning is in Ideation.

03 / Research / Competitive Analysis

Everything either costs too much to start, or shows you clothes you don't own.

Four kinds of product try to help. The fifth option is no product at all. What's missing is one that answers the question at the moment you ask it.

A

Wardrobe apps

Looked at: Whering, Acloset, Indyx, Stylebook

The most complete answer. Add your whole wardrobe and the app builds outfits from what you own.

The gap

The setup comes first. Photographing and tagging a whole wardrobe takes hours, before you get a single answer.

B

Pinterest

Looked at: Pinterest search and boards

Unmatched for inspiration, and the only place that shows what a colour combination looks like on a real person.

The gap

It's all clothes you don't own. It shows what looks good, never what you can wear tonight. That gap is what ShadeMatch is for.

C

Marketplace styling

Looked at: Myntra “Complete the Look”, Amazon “Style with”

Good recommendations, shown right when you're ready to buy.

The gap

Every suggestion is something for sale. The app isn't asking "what goes with this?", it's asking "what else can we sell this person?" Shoppers know it, and a suggestion sitting next to a buy button gets trusted less, even when it's right.

D

General AI chat

Looked at: ChatGPT and Gemini with a photo

Free, immediate, capable. Upload a photo of a shirt, ask what goes with it, get a reasonable answer. The strongest competitor in the category and the least discussed.

The gap

Nothing persists. No memory of your wardrobe, no record of yesterday's question, no visual output, just text. It also needs the user to know they can ask, and to phrase it well. A capable tool with no product around it.

04 / Research / Voices

I read what people said about the wardrobe apps they quit.

Method

No interviews for this one. I used two sources instead.

Source 1

Community threads

AI pulled threads from fashion advice communities. I looked for how people describe the moment of not knowing what goes with something.

Source 2

App reviews

AI pulled the 2 and 3 star reviews of the wardrobe apps I looked at, from the App Store and Play Store. I read through them. This is where people explain why they quit.

2 and 3 starThe real reasons
1 and 5 starNoise
What I got

Honest opinions at volume, and a clear view of why competitors lose people.

What I missed

No follow-up questions. Only people who bothered to post. The quiet majority who used an app happily never show up.

Three clusters

Cluster 01

“I don't trust my own eye.”

What I saw

The main feeling is self doubt, not confusion. People feel they are missing a skill everyone else has. They own clothes they like and have never worn, and can't say why.

What they need

→A second opinion with a reason. Not a verdict, an explanation they can carry forward.

Cluster 02  ★ Key insight

“The setup costs more than the problem.”

What I saw

Mostly from review mining. People start excited, upload fifteen to thirty items, then quit before the app has done anything useful. The work comes before the value, every time.

Excited  →  15 to 30 items uploaded  →  Quits
What they need

→Value at item one, not item one hundred.

Cluster 03  ★ Key insight

“Everything is trying to sell me something.”

What I saw

A weary suspicion of styling advice attached to commerce. People discount recommendations automatically when a buy button is present, not because the advice is wrong but because they can't tell whether it's for them or for the retailer.

What they need

→Advice with no motive. The recommendation has to be visibly disinterested to be usable at all. This cluster directly contradicts a decision in the original build.

The business case

India's fashion ecommerce market was worth roughly $25.6B in 2025, apparel at 54% of it, Myntra alone at about $12.5B GMV. It's not a small pond, and there's obvious money in sending qualified traffic into it.

Why Aditi is primary

Rohan is the more commercially attractive user. He's at the point of purchase, already spending, one click from an affiliate link. Designing for him monetises immediately. I prioritised Aditi anyway, for three reasons.

Frequency. Aditi has the problem every morning. Rohan has it when he shops. A daily problem builds a habit, an occasional one builds a bookmark.

Trust. Designing for Rohan pulls the product toward commerce, and Cluster 3 says commerce is exactly what makes styling advice unusable.

Coverage. A product that answers “what goes with this thing I own” can answer “what goes with this thing I'm about to buy” with almost no extra design. The reverse isn't true, because a purchase time tool has nothing to say at 8am on a Tuesday.

Aditi became the design target. Rohan became the expansion case: not designed for now, and not designed against.

Redefined challenge

My first version treated this as a colour problem and solved it with a colour formula. It isn't one. It's a confidence problem. People don't just need an answer, they need an answer they understand.

How might we help people wear more of what they own, by giving them an instant second opinion on a single garment, one that explains itself, needs no setup, and has nothing to sell them?
Built for: the moment of getting dressed, holding one item Not for: wardrobe management, shopping, or building a styling habit
06 / Design / Ideation

The hardest part was subtraction.

Most design projects start with nothing and add. This one started full. ShadeMatch already did five things, and my job was to decide which were the product and which were just clutter.

The three questions everything had to pass

5 features as built
Gate 01

Does it serve the moment of getting dressed?

Gate 02

Does it cost the user anything before it gives them something?

Gate 03

Does it survive the trust test?

4 kept, two of them rebuilt
The audit below is these three gates applied to what shipped. One feature failed outright and left the core.
01

Does this serve the moment of getting dressed? Not shopping, not browsing. If a feature's best use case was “while browsing Myntra,” it was out of the core however well it worked.

02

Does it cost the user anything before it gives them something? Setup cost is what kills this category. Accounts, uploads, quizzes and onboarding were out unless they earned their cost inside the same session.

03

Does it survive the trust test? Would Aditi believe this recommendation if she knew exactly how the product made money? If not, it was damaging the product even while working correctly.

Auditing what already existed

Feature, as builtVerdictReasoning
Single garment photo capture Core, keep The strongest thing in the product. Near zero setup is the entire competitive position.
Colour combination suggestions Rebuild output Right feature, wrong presentation. Colours without reasons answer the question once instead of teaching the user to answer it.
Amazon, Myntra and Flipkart links Cut from core Fails question 3 outright. Attaches a commercial motive to advice that only works without one. The most consequential cut in the project.
Pinterest link Keep, promote Underrated in the original build. The only thing answering “what does this look like on a person.”

Why cutting the marketplace links was the right call

The decision I argued with myself about most, so here's the reasoning.

The case for keeping them

They're the only revenue in the product, and affiliate programmes are trivially easy to join in a $25.6B market. They're occasionally useful too, because sometimes the honest answer is that you own nothing that works.

The case for cutting them, which won

Cluster 3 is unambiguous. Trust here isn't a nice to have, it is the product.

The entire reason Aditi would choose this over Myntra's “Complete the Look” is the absence of a motive.

It snowballs, too. Once money depends on clicks, every later decision starts leaning toward the click. Cutting the links removes that pressure before it starts.

Where they went instead: a secondary path, entered deliberately. When a wardrobe genuinely doesn't contain a match, then seeing what exists commercially is a service rather than an ad, because the user asked.

What got cut, and what never made it in

IdeaVerdictWhy it didn't survive
Full wardrobe upload and outfit generation Cut The category's cause of death. Would have traded the product's one advantage for its competitors' one weakness.
Account and login before first use Cut Fails question 2. Value has to arrive before commitment, not after.
Style quiz onboarding Cut Six taps before a useful output, for a user who hasn't agreed she has a problem. Every tap is a leak.
Social feed and outfit sharing Cut Solves a problem Aditi doesn't have. She isn't short of inspiration, she's short of confidence about her own cupboard.
Skin tone colour analysis Deferred Valuable, and a different product. Needs accuracy I can't validate and carries real harm risk if wrong.
Occasion filter for work, casual and evening Deferred Good idea, but adds a decision to a flow whose promise is removing one. Second version.

What the ideation changed

Two decisions came out of this pass that the original build had skipped.

The count came down. Early versions showed eight, ten, twelve options. It looked more capable and performed worse. Aditi has a two minute window and low confidence, and twelve options hands the same decision back in a new wrapper. Three.

The empty state became a real screen. What happens when nothing you own works? The build had no answer, and it isn't an edge case. For an accumulated wardrobe it may be the most common outcome.

07 / Design / Evaluation

Checking my own app against what I'd learned.

ShadeMatch had no recruited testers behind this section either. What I ran was a structured self evaluation, walking through the live app screen by screen and checking it against the research findings from the competitive analysis, the voices, and the personas.

Method

Three questions per screen:

01What would a first time user expect before opening this?
02Could they say what the app just told them, in their own words?
03Would they actually wear it? Not “is this correct,” but “did it change what someone would do.”
To be honest about it: this was me checking my own app against my research, not a test with real users. It's good at catching confusing screens. It can't tell me whether anyone's habits changed. That needs the diary study below.

What it caught

1

The output gives an answer with no reason attached.

A combination arrives with no explanation, which doesn't help a user whose real problem is confidence.

2

The shopping links change how the whole app reads.

Once you see them, every suggestion looks like a sales pitch. This is exactly what Cluster 3 warned me about, and I only saw it by looking at my own app like a stranger.

3

A swatch doesn't answer “what does that look like on me.”

The Pinterest link, treated as a minor extra, is the only thing that actually answers this.

Revised design goals

The evaluation didn't change what the product is. It changed four things about how it has to behave.

Original goalRevised goalTrigger
Suggest colours that match → Suggest colours that match and say why in one line. Every output must teach, not just answer Failure 1
Help users find matching items → Help users find matching items they already own. Commerce only on request, never volunteered Failure 2
Show the recommended combination → Let them see it on a person before committing. Abstract palettes don't convert to worn outfits Failure 3
08 / Design / The Flow

One photo to an answer.

The whole product is one short loop. Aditi is at her cupboard with two minutes, and every extra screen is a chance to lose her.

01 Capture
02 Detect
03 Recommend
04 Visualise
The whole product in one row. Four screens, from photo to answer.
Step 01

Capture

What happens

You land on one screen. Take a photo with your camera, or drag and drop an image. No home screen, no login, no setup.

The design decision

No account until someone has a reason to want one. Every extra screen before the photo loses people, especially the ones who don't yet think they have a problem. Landing straight on the photo screen says: this costs nothing to try.

What it protects

The only structural advantage over wardrobe apps, and worth more than everything an account would enable.

Step 02

Detection

What happens

The garment's dominant colour is identified and shown back, named rather than just swatched. “Rust orange.” “Sage.”

The design decision

Show what the app saw before it recommends anything. If it got the garment wrong, everything after is wrong, and the user should catch that early. Naming the colour helps too. A user who learns her shirt is “rust”, not “orange-ish”, keeps that.

What it protects

Trust. When an AI app shows its work where that's easy, people believe it more when it can't.

Step 03

Recommendation

What happens

Three colours for each piece that goes with the one you photographed.

The decision: three, not twelve

A full palette looks more capable and performs worse. Three options is a recommendation. Twelve is a colour picker.

Step 04

Visualise

What happens

One tap opens Pinterest, already searched for the specific combination, showing real people wearing it.

What I planned, and what I shipped

The original plan was to show both garments together in one image, so you could actually see the combination instead of imagining it.

I hit technical issues getting that to work reliably.

So I fell back to something simpler: a direct link to Pinterest, showing real people wearing that exact colour combination. Not as tailored as a custom generated image. But faster to ship, and it gets the person to a real picture today.

The planNever built

technical issues
What shipped
A link that opens Pinterest, already searched for the combination.
The risk, acknowledged

Sending people to another app means some won't come back. That's the price of the fallback. Getting the image inside ShadeMatch is the next thing I'd fix.

09 / Evaluation / Measuring Success

The easiest number to track would have ruined this app.

The easiest thing to count is clicks to Amazon, Myntra and Flipkart. It's exact and it ties straight to money. And chasing it would undo everything that makes the app work.

North star metric

The one number that matters most
Percentage of sessions where the user wears an outfit they wouldn't otherwise have worn.

Not sessions. Not time in app. Not suggestions generated. All of those rise when a product is confusing, and the goal is a garment leaving the shelf.

The honest caveat: this is hard to instrument, because you can't observe what someone wore. The usable proxy is a “wore this” confirmation on a return visit, which is its own design problem, since adding a check in to a product promising zero friction is a contradiction. My answer is one optional tap surfaced only on return, accepting incomplete data over a degraded experience. A compromise I'd want to test.

Supporting metrics

Design goalMetricTargetWhy this one
Value before commitment % of first sessions reaching a recommendation > 85% If people drop before the first answer, the zero setup promise failed at its only job
Speed of answer Median seconds, launch to first recommendation < 30s Aditi's window is two minutes. Slower than this loses to the mirror.
Comprehension, not correctness % of sessions where the reason line is read or expanded > 50% A reason nobody reads is a reason that isn't working
Confidence compounding % of returning users applying a past combination without rescanning > 25% The strongest signal available, because the user learned something and kept it
Trust Suggestion acceptance rate, with against without commerce present < 5% Directly measures the Cluster 3 contamination effect
★ Real value Distinct garments involved in accepted suggestions, per user, per month Rising The only metric that measures the actual problem moving rather than a proxy for it

How I'd validate

Qual

Usability, moderated. 5 to 8 people matching Aditi, on their own wardrobe. Two questions: does the reason line change whether they'd wear it, and do they notice the missing shopping links.

Longitudinal

Diary study, 2 weeks. 6 participants logging what they wore. The only method that can answer whether unworn garments actually moved, which is the entire point.

Quant

A/B on commerce placement. The highest value test available: volunteered shopping links against empty state only, measured on acceptance rate rather than clicks. The decision I'd most want proven right or wrong.

10 / Evaluation / Results

What I can claim, and what I can't.

ShadeMatch is live but it isn't a business, and there's no meaningful usage data behind it. Here's what the work actually produced.

What the audit showed me

✓
The zero setup entry point is the competitive position. It survived the landscape analysis as the one thing no competitor offers, and every decision downstream now protects it.
✓
The output still needs a reason, not just an answer. This is the change I'd make next. It goes after the real problem, confidence, rather than the surface one, colour. The app doesn't do it yet.
✓
Shopping links next to advice make it feel like an ad. This is the finding that made me cut a feature. It comes from the reviews and my own check, and I haven't tested it with users yet.
✓
I got two of the five features wrong the first time. I treated Pinterest as a bonus when it's central, and treated the shopping links as central when they hurt. Not bad for a build with no research behind it, and exactly why I did this exercise.

What is not validated

○
That anyone wears anything differently. The entire premise, that this moves garments from unworn to worn, needs a two week diary study. The only thing that would truly matter, and the thing I don't have.
○
The colour matching itself. I've assessed the recommendations as plausible, not validated them against colour theory or tested them across skin tones, lighting and fabric texture, all of which change the answer.
○
The business model. Removing the only revenue is defensible on trust grounds and untested commercially. Trust first and monetise later is a strategy, not a plan.
○
Retention. A product designed to be needed less over time has a retention problem built into its own goal.

The outcome

What this project delivered

A defensible competitive position, an audit that cut one of five features and reworked the rest, a decision record for every call, and a measurement framework with counter metrics.

What it didn't

Proof that anyone's wardrobe changed.

11 / Reflection

What auditing my own product taught me.

01

When building gets quick, I have to add the thinking back on purpose.

Several decisions in ShadeMatch were made by default rather than badly. The marketplace links weren't a monetisation strategy, they were the obvious thing to include. The filter label was never a naming decision at all. When building is expensive, the expense forces justification, because you don't spend three weeks on a feature you can't defend. When it takes an afternoon, nothing forces it, and the rigour has to come from somewhere else on purpose. I don't think I'd have learned that in the abstract.

02

I had an edge and didn't know it.

ShadeMatch's strongest point is that it only asks for one photo, which is exactly what its competitors get wrong. But I never chose that. I stumbled into it. You can't tell getting it right by luck from getting it right on purpose until someone asks you why. The research turned a lucky accident into a choice I can defend, and I know not to give it up.

03

The same feature is helpful or manipulative depending on who starts the conversation.

Shopping links beside a recommendation are an ad. The identical links, after the app has honestly said nothing you own works, are a service. Only the initiator changed. I used to evaluate features on what they do. Now the first question is who asked for it, because that sets the meaning more than the function does.

04

Choose what to measure before anyone starts counting.

Once the easy number is in place, it starts winning every argument. So the harder, blurrier number that measures real value has to be chosen early.

05

Success here means the user needs me less.

The real outcome is that Aditi learns which colours work and gradually stops opening the app. Good for her, structurally terrible for a retention metric, and I don't have a clean answer. I've stopped treating it as a flaw in my reasoning and started treating it as the actual product question: what does a tool designed to make itself unnecessary sell, and to whom?

What I'd do next

The gap between this case study and a real product is one study: two weeks, six people, a diary of what they actually wore. Everything here is reasoning, and reasoning is cheap. The only question that matters is whether a garment that sat unworn for two years came off the shelf.

The second thing I'd do is audit the colour engine itself. Writing this, I noticed I'd spent weeks interrogating every decision around the recommendation and none on whether the recommendation is any good. A pointed lesson about where I'm comfortable thinking and where I'm not.
